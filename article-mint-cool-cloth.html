Fresh Mint Cloth: A Summer Household Practice

My grandmother kept mint in a cracked clay pot on the kitchen windowsill. On hot afternoons, she picked a few leaves, rubbed them once between her palms, and dropped them into an enamel basin of cool water. A folded cotton cloth went in after the leaves. The basin stayed near the back door, where the floor was shaded.

A pot on the sill

The mint pot sat on the third window ledge from the left, where morning sun reached it and afternoon glare did not. My grandmother did not fuss over it. She watered it with leftover water from rinsing rice, the same way she watered the scallion ends and sweet potato vines she kept in jars.

Mint was useful because it was already there. It did not need buying each time. It did not sit in the market basket until it wilted. A few leaves could be pinched off and the plant would keep growing. In a household where rice water was saved instead of poured away, that kind of plant had an obvious place.

The cloth by the door

On the hottest afternoons, she filled the enamel basin from the tap. The mint leaves went in first. She rubbed them between her fingers until the water smelled green. Then the folded cotton cloth went in, and the basin was set on the floor by the back door.

If someone came in from the yard with a flushed face and a shirt stuck to the back, she wrung out the cloth and handed it over without comment. Forehead first. Then the back of the neck. Then across both wrists. "The wrists show the heat first," she said. The cloth went back into the basin after each use.

There was no name for this practice. It was just something she did with mint, water, and cotton cloth. In the evening, the same house might use a warm towel compress for tired eyes. One cloth was kept cool in a basin. Another was wrung from hot water. The difference was season and hour.

Too small for a recipe

This is the kind of custom that rarely enters books. It is too small. A pot, a leaf, a cloth, some water. It fits between other work: rinsing vegetables, watching a pot, bringing in laundry before the afternoon air changes.

Fresh mint cloth sits beside other household habits that used what was already near at hand: lotus root water from a market vegetable, rice water from washing grain, a towel folded over the eyes at the end of the day. The materials are plain. The record is the sequence of use.

A quick word

This article records a folk household practice for cultural interest only. In the households described here, the cloth stayed off broken or sensitive skin and was set aside if it irritated the skin.
