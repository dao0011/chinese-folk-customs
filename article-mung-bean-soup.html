Mung Bean Soup for Summer Afternoons — A Chinese Folk Cooler

Every summer afternoon at three o'clock, my grandmother would have a pot of mung bean soup on the stove. Not sweetened. Not salted. Nothing added but water and dried mung beans, cooked until they split open and released their pale green starch into the water, then left to cool until the surface was still and the pot was only slightly warm to the touch. She'd ladle a bowl, lean against the kitchen doorframe, and drink it standing up — three or four unhurried minutes, the bowl held in both hands, her eyes resting on nothing in particular. Then she'd rinse the bowl, set it on the drying rack, and go back to whatever task she'd paused. It wasn't called a break. It was just three o'clock, and three o'clock was when you drank the mung bean soup.

Summer in a Sichuan kitchen

To understand why mung bean soup mattered, you have to understand the heat. Not the kind of heat that makes you uncomfortable on a July afternoon in an air-conditioned city. The kind of heat that lives in a Sichuan summer kitchen: damp, heavy, unmoving, the air so thick with humidity that a dish towel hung to dry at noon is still damp at dinner. The kind of heat that makes you not want to eat.

In that kind of heat, the body wants less of everything — less food, less movement, less noise. The kitchen, which in winter was the coziest room in the house, became in summer the one you avoided. My grandmother cooked less in July and August. She made congee in the early morning, before the sun had climbed above the roofline. She stir-fried quickly, standing as far from the wok as the spatula allowed. And she always — always — had a pot of mung bean soup cooling somewhere, either on the back of the stove or on the windowsill where a cross-breeze might find it.

Mung bean soup wasn't a food in the way that lunch was a food. It was more like a tool — something you reached for the way you reached for a paper fan or a cold cloth, not because you were hungry but because you were hot and the body wanted something that registered as "cooling" from the inside. Every culture has its versions of this: the salted lassi in India, the chilled cucumber soup in Eastern Europe, the glass of cold barley tea in Japan. The mung bean — the way chrysanthemum flowers stored at the bottom of a tea tin belonged to autumn — was the summer plant in Chinese folk thinking: not because anyone made a formal argument for it, but because generations of people noticed that drinking its cooking water on a hot day made them feel less oppressed by the heat. Mung beans and winter melon tea were the two pillars of summer kitchen cooling in our house — mung beans for everyday drinking, winter melon for when the throat felt parched. That was enough. No one needed a study.

What mung bean soup actually is

Let me be precise about what we're talking about, because "mung bean soup" means different things in different contexts and I don't want to describe the wrong thing.

This is lv dou tang (绿豆汤) in its simplest, most stripped-down form. Just dried mung beans and water. Nothing else. No sugar. No salt. No aromatics. The beans are small and green and oval — about the size of a lentil, but brighter, the color of new leaves. They cook faster than most dried beans. Thirty minutes of simmering is enough to split their skins and release their starch, turning the cooking water a translucent, faintly greenish-amber. You can eat the beans at the bottom of the bowl if you want — they're soft and slightly mealy — but the point of the soup is the liquid, not the solids.

This is not the sweetened mung bean soup served as a dessert in Cantonese restaurants — the one with rock sugar and dried tangerine peel and sometimes sago pearls, chilled and served in a glass bowl with a spoon. That's a different thing, a treat, meant to be eaten cold and sweet after a banquet meal. My grandmother's version was savory — though "savory" is also the wrong word, since it contained nothing that would register as salty or umami either. It was simply itself. The taste of mung beans and water, which is to say: a taste so mild it barely qualifies as a taste, closer to the memory of grass than to anything you'd describe as "flavor" in a restaurant review.

How she made it

Rinse half a cup of dried mung beans in cold water — just a quick rinse to remove any dust from storage. No soaking. No overnight preparation. This was one of the things she liked about mung beans: they asked almost nothing of you in advance. Unlike the sticky rice for zongzi, which had to be soaked for hours, or the dried shiitakes that had to be rehydrated before they could be sliced, mung beans went from cupboard to pot without ceremony.

Into a medium pot went the rinsed beans and about six cups of water. She never measured precisely — the water came up to about two knuckles above the beans, the same informal metric she used for cooking rice. Bring to a boil. The foam would rise immediately, a pale greenish scum that she'd skim off with a flat spoon. "This is the dust," she'd say, though it wasn't dust — it was just the loose starch and protein fragments that any dried bean releases when it first meets boiling water. Every cook in every culture skims their beans. It's one of those gestures that must have been discovered independently a thousand times in a thousand kitchens.

Once the foam was skimmed, she'd lower the heat to a simmer, set the lid slightly askew so steam could escape, and leave it. For about thirty minutes. She never set a timer. She'd check once or twice — not by looking at the clock but by peering into the pot to see whether the beans had split. A mung bean that's ready has a tiny crack along its seam, like a zipper just starting to open. The water around it turns faintly cloudy, the starch beginning to dissolve.

When all the beans had split — or most of them; a few stubborn ones always stayed whole — she'd turn off the heat. Then came the part that was hardest for me to learn: she didn't serve it. Not yet. She let it sit. The pot stayed on the stove, lid off, for an hour or more, until it was barely warm. If the kitchen had a breeze from the window, she'd move it there. The cooling was part of the cooking. Hot mung bean soup is fine; room-temperature mung bean soup is the thing itself. The mung bean's character — what Chinese folk tradition calls its "cooling nature" (liang, 凉) — is felt most clearly when the soup is not hot.

The three-o'clock ritual

Time mattered. She always drank it around three in the afternoon — the deepest part of the afternoon heat, when the sun was past its peak but the walls of the house had been absorbing warmth since morning and were now radiating it back into every room. Three o'clock in a Sichuan summer is a slow hour. Siesta hour in some cultures. Tea hour in others. In my grandmother's kitchen, it was mung bean soup hour.

She didn't sit down. This was the detail I noticed most. She had chairs — the same wooden stools we sat on for every meal — but when she drank her mung bean soup she stood. At the kitchen door, looking out at the courtyard or the vegetable patch or whatever happened to be in her line of sight. The bowl held in both hands — not because it was heavy but because the coolness of the ceramic against her palms was part of the experience. She'd take small sips. Not gulping. A bowl of mung bean soup lasted three or four minutes.

I asked her once, when I was old enough to notice patterns and young enough to still ask obvious questions, why she always drank it standing up. She looked at me as if the question had never occurred to her — which it probably hadn't — and said, "Because I'm not sitting down." That was the full explanation. She wasn't resting. She was pausing. There's a difference. A rest says: I'm done for now. A pause says: I'm still in the middle of things, but this moment needs to happen before the next one begins. The standing position was the boundary between the pause and the rest. It meant the soup was part of the workday, not a departure from it.

Some of my clearest memories of her are in that posture: leaning in the kitchen doorway, bowl in both hands, gaze unfocused, the afternoon light coming in low through the window behind her. She was doing nothing. She was doing exactly what she needed to be doing. The mung bean soup gave her a reason to stop for three minutes — without having to call it stopping.

Mung bean soup vs. mung bean congee

They're not the same thing. I want to draw the distinction clearly because I've seen them conflated, and my grandmother would have corrected anyone who mixed them up.

Mung bean soup — lv dou tang — is thin. It's a drink. The beans are there because they're what infused the water with its character, but you're not trying to eat a bowl of beans. If someone in my grandmother's house asked for tang (soup), they got liquid, with maybe a spoonful of beans settled at the bottom.

Mung bean congee — lv dou zhou — is thick. It involves rice. You cook mung beans and rice together in more water than you'd use for regular rice, but less than you'd use for soup, until everything breaks down into a porridge. This is a meal. You eat it with a spoon, often with a small dish of pickled vegetables on the side. It's breakfast food, or sickbed food, not something you drink standing up at three in the afternoon.

The distinction mattered to her because it was a distinction about purpose. The soup was for the heat. The congee was for hunger. Two different problems, two different answers, from the same small green bean. She liked things that could serve more than one purpose — which is also why she kept soap pods in a jar by the sink and milky rice water on the bathroom shelf. In a household where nothing was wasted, a plant that could do two jobs was better than one that could do only one.

The taste of nothing much

If you've never tasted unsweetened, unsalted mung bean soup, here is the closest description I can give: it tastes the way a field of grass smells just after it's been cut — green and clean and faintly sweet, but so faintly that you wonder if the sweetness is really there or if you're imagining it. It's not bitter. It's not sour. It's not salty. It occupies a sensory category that most modern palates, trained on sweetened drinks and flavored waters and beverages engineered in laboratories to hit precise notes, don't quite know what to do with.

The first sip registers as coolness — even at room temperature, there's a sensation of something settling and quieting, the way a cold stone feels on your palm on a hot day. The flavor arrives late, a half-second behind the temperature, and it's so mild that you have to pay attention to notice it. My grandmother didn't notice it. She'd been drinking it for sixty years. The taste was so familiar that it had become invisible — the way the taste of your own tap water is invisible to you but strange to a visitor from another city.

The beans at the bottom of the bowl are soft and bland — not unpleasant, just neutral, the way cooked lentils are neutral. My grandmother ate them if she was hungry. Skipped them if she wasn't. They weren't the point. The point was the liquid, and the liquid was the color of pale jade, and it tasted like a pause.

Who else made this

My grandmother's version — beans, water, nothing else — was the baseline. But as I grew up and talked to people from other households and other regions, I learned that almost everyone's family had a slightly different mung bean soup, and each variation was defended as the obviously correct one.

Some families added a small piece of dried tangerine peel (chen pi) to the pot. The peel contributed a faint citrus bitterness that cut through the bean starch and made the soup feel lighter on the tongue. This was common in Guangdong and Guangxi, where dried tangerine peel is a pantry staple and people are particular about the way bitter and sweet and neutral flavors balance each other.

Some families added dried lily bulb (bai he) — not for flavor, really, but because lily bulb was considered another "cooling" ingredient, and the two together seemed like they belonged to the same season. The lily bulb disintegrated during cooking, adding a slight sweetness and a tiny bit of body to the liquid. My grandmother sometimes did this, but only when she happened to have dried lily bulb on hand — she didn't buy it specifically for mung bean soup.

Some families — mostly in the north — added a tiny pinch of salt. Not enough to make the soup taste salty. Just enough to round out the flavor, the way a pinch of salt in cake batter doesn't make the cake salty but makes it taste more like itself. My grandmother didn't do this. She was strict about the "nothing added" principle, though she couldn't articulate why. It was just that her mother hadn't added salt, and neither had hers.

The Cantonese dessert version — sweetened, sometimes with coconut milk — was the furthest departure. My grandmother considered this an entirely different food that happened to share an ingredient. The way a steamed fish and a fish ball soup are both fish, but no one would confuse them.

I make it now, standing at my own counter

I don't have a kitchen door to lean against — just a galley kitchen in an apartment with a window that faces another building. I don't have a courtyard to look out at, and three o'clock is usually a video call or a deadline. But I still make mung bean soup in the summer, and when I do, I find myself standing at the counter to drink it. Not because I'm in a hurry. Not because I've forgotten to sit down. But because sitting down would make it into something else — a meal, an interlude, a formal declaration of rest — and what I'm after is the thing my grandmother had: a pause that doesn't announce itself, a three-minute gap in the middle of the afternoon that keeps the day moving without breaking it in half.

The soup tastes the same. That's the thing that still surprises me. I use beans from a package with a brand name on it, cooked on an induction burner instead of a gas stove, in a kitchen that looks nothing like hers. But the mung bean doesn't know any of that. It splits open and releases its starch into the water exactly the way it did in her kitchen and in the kitchens of all the women before her. The plant hasn't changed. Only everything around it has.

Sometimes I add a piece of dried tangerine peel — a small heresy, a nod to the Guangxi variation I learned about from a colleague. It's good. My grandmother would probably have approved, in the same way she approved of small improvements that didn't disrespect the original. What she wouldn't have approved of is sugar — turning a summer survival tool into a dessert. But I don't add sugar. So I think we're still on the same page.

Materials

  • Dried mung beans (lv dou, 绿豆) — small, green, oval. Available at any Asian grocery store, most well-stocked supermarkets, and online. Look for beans that are uniformly green without gray patches or insect holes. Store in a sealed jar in a cool cupboard; they keep for a year or more.
  • Water — about 6 cups per half-cup of dried beans.
  • A medium pot with a lid.
  • Optional: a small piece of dried tangerine peel (chen pi, 陈皮), a small handful of dried lily bulb (bai he, 百合), or a pinch of salt — depending on regional preference.

Steps — Basic mung bean soup

  1. Rinse half a cup of dried mung beans briefly in cold water. No need to soak.
  2. Place the rinsed beans in a pot with about 6 cups of water. If using optional additions (tangerine peel, lily bulb), add them now.
  3. Bring to a boil over high heat. A greenish foam will rise to the surface — skim it off with a spoon and discard.
  4. Reduce heat to low. Set the lid slightly askew on the pot so steam can escape.
  5. Simmer for 25–35 minutes, until the beans have split open along their seams and the water is a translucent, pale greenish-amber.
  6. Turn off the heat. Remove the lid and let the pot sit for at least an hour — until the soup is room temperature or only slightly warm. If you have a breezy windowsill, this is the time to use it.
  7. Ladle into a bowl. Drink the liquid. Eat the beans at the bottom or leave them — both are correct.

A few things to keep in mind

Mung beans are a food — a legume, no different in category from lentils or chickpeas. If you have a known sensitivity or allergy to legumes, skip this one. Most people tolerate mung beans without issue, but if you've never eaten them before, start with a small amount.

When Chinese folk tradition describes mung beans as "cooling" (liang, 凉), this is a cultural classification within traditional Chinese dietary thinking, not a statement about measurable physical effects. It refers to a long folk observation that mung bean soup feels soothing in hot weather. The explanation is cultural; the feeling is real; the two should not be confused for each other.

Unsweetened bean cooking water has a very mild, grassy, neutral taste. If you're accustomed to flavored beverages, it may seem bland at first. That blandness is the feature, not the bug. It's what generations of people reached for when they wanted something that would cool them down without asking their palate to do any work.
