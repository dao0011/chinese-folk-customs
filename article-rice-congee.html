Plain Rice Congee: The Bowl That Shows Up When Nothing Else Will Do

In any Chinese household, if you ask what the most comforting thing to eat is on a tired morning or a cold evening, the answer is almost always the same: a bowl of congee. Not the fancy restaurant versions with century egg and pork — just rice and water, cooked until the grains lose their shape and become something between a soup and a porridge. The kind of bowl a grandmother makes without measuring anything.

Congee is maybe the most versatile food in Chinese domestic life. It appears at breakfast with pickled vegetables and a fried dough stick. It appears beside someone's bed when they're recovering from a cold, served plain with maybe a drizzle of soy sauce. It appears as a late-night snack for a student studying for exams, or for a grandparent who can't manage heavier food anymore. In every case, the message is the same: this is the gentlest thing I can offer you.

What gets me is how universal it is. Chinese people living abroad consistently report that one of the first things they cook after a long trip home — or after a homesick evening in a foreign country — is a pot of congee. It's not about nutrition in any calculated way. It's about the smell of rice cooking slowly, the sound of a gentle bubble from the stove, and the warmth of a plain white bowl in both hands.

My grandfather had a saying that I've never forgotten: "Sip a few mouthfuls of soup before a meal, and you'll live to ninety-nine." In our family, congee was that soup — the gentle start to every meal, especially when someone needed looking after.

A habit that travels by watching, not by reading

Most people never learn to make congee from a recipe. They learn by watching. A kid sits on the kitchen counter while a parent rinses rice, adds water to a pot, sets the flame low, and goes about morning tasks. The kid learns the rhythm: rinse, add water, wait, stir occasionally, wait some more. Years later, standing in their own kitchen somewhere else entirely, they find themselves doing exactly the same thing — rinsing rice, adding water, setting the flame low — without ever having written down a single measurement.

That hands-on transmission is why congee varies so much from family to family. One household uses one part rice to twelve parts water. Another uses one to eight. Some soak the rice first. Some don't. Some add a tiny piece of ginger to the pot. Some keep it perfectly plain. None of these versions is more "authentic" than another — they're all equally authentic because the tradition is defined by the act of making it, not by the proportions on a page.

The beauty of extreme simplicity

The beauty of congee is its absolute blankness. Rice, water, time. That's the entire recipe. Yet within that simplicity lives an enormous range of texture and feeling: some families prefer it thick enough to stand a spoon upright, others like it thin and drinkable. Some stir constantly. Others barely touch it for hours. The differences are regional, seasonal, and deeply personal — which is why this article describes the habit in broad strokes rather than prescribing exact ratios.

Materials

  • White rice — short-grain or medium-grain works best. Long-grain works too but yields a thinner texture.
  • Water — roughly eight to twelve times the volume of rice, depending how thick you like it
  • A heavy-bottomed pot or a rice cooker with a porridge setting
  • A wooden spoon or spatula for occasional stirring
  • Optional: a small piece of ginger, a pinch of salt, or a drop of sesame oil for serving

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice in a bowl of water, drain, repeat once or twice until the water runs mostly clear.
  2. Put the rice in a pot and add water. A common starting point is one cup of rice to ten cups of water.
  3. Bring to a gentle boil over medium heat, then reduce to the lowest simmer your stove can manage.
  4. Stir occasionally — every ten or fifteen minutes is plenty — to keep the rice from sticking to the bottom.
  5. Cook for 45 minutes to two hours, depending how soft and thick you want it. Longer cooking gives a smoother, creamier texture.
  6. Taste and adjust: a pinch of salt if you want, or serve plain with side dishes.

Common additions (all optional)

While plain congee is the baseline, a lot of families add small touches:

  • A few slices of ginger, added at the start — for a faint warmth in the background. Fresh ginger tea works the same way as a gentle starter before meals.
  • A handful of washed red dates or goji berries — for subtle sweetness
  • A pinch of salt or a drop of light soy sauce — at the table, not during cooking
  • A drizzle of sesame oil and chopped scallions — right before serving
  • Sweet potato cubes, pumpkin pieces, or taro — added early for a heartier bowl

None of these additions changes what the dish fundamentally is. Congee with ginger is still congee. Congee with sweet potato is still congee. The habit is the bowl itself, not what you put in it.

Comfort and safety

Congee is, at its core, cooked rice and water — the same ingredients in virtually every rice-eating culture on earth. The main safety concern is temperature: like any hot food, steam can burn, so let the bowl sit for a few minutes before eating. If you're using a rice cooker, follow the manufacturer's instructions and don't overfill past the marked line.

If you have dietary restrictions related to rice consumption — diabetes, concerns about cross-contamination for celiac purposes, or any condition monitored by a healthcare professional — check with them before making congee a regular thing. This article describes a folk food habit, not a dietary recommendation for anyone in particular.

Who might want to check with a professional first

While rice congee is one of the most widely eaten foods in the world, individual circumstances vary. If a professional has advised you to limit carbohydrate intake, or if you have a rice allergy or sensitivity, this habit may not fit your situation. When in doubt, ask the same person who guides your food choices — they can tell you whether a bowl of rice porridge makes sense in your routine.

The Huangdi Neijing states plainly that grain is the basis of nourishment. Congee — rice cooked to its softest, most humble form — echoes that old idea of grain as the foundation of daily meals. If you live outside China

You don't need special equipment to make congee. Any pot with a lid and a stove will work. Rice cookers with a "porridge" setting make the process even simpler. Look for short-grain or medium-grain white rice at your grocery — jasmine rice is a common choice and widely available. The rest is just water and patience.

If your first batch turns out too thick or too thin, welcome to the club. Adjust the water ratio next time. Congee forgives generously — it is, after all, the most forgiving dish in the Chinese kitchen.
