Ginger on the Scalp

My grandmother's bathroom shelf held three things: a toothbrush, a glass, and a piece of ginger. Not the big cooking pieces from the kitchen — this was a small knob, cut off and set aside, wrapped in a scrap of plastic wrap, kept next to the soap dish. Every Sunday, after washing her hair, she'd stand in front of the mirror, slice a fresh face on the ginger with a paring knife, bend forward, and press it into her scalp — especially the two corners above her forehead where the hair had thinned and you could see the pink skin beneath. She called it "waking the roots up."

Fresh cut, every time

The ginger had to be cut right before she used it. Never in advance. "Once the cut dries, it's useless," she said. Each Sunday she'd slice off the dried end from the previous week, exposing a new pale yellow face, wet enough to leave a smear on her skin. She'd rub it into her hairline in short, firm strokes — left temple, right temple, the strip above the forehead, the little bare patches just above her ears. When the cut surface stopped leaving juice behind, she'd slice it again. One knob would last maybe three or four cuts before it was too small to hold.

After a minute, the rubbed spots would start to feel warm. She said it was the ginger doing its work. Sometimes she'd have me do the back of her head — the spot she couldn't see in the mirror. I was short enough that I had to stand on my toes. I'd take the ginger from her hand and press it into her hairline at the nape of her neck while she bent forward over the sink. The smell was sharp and clean — raw ginger mixed with whatever plain soap she'd just washed her hair with. She said the ginger smell would stay in her hair all day. "Better than perfume," she'd say, and I believed her. In the kitchen, she used the same ginger sliced into hot water after dinner — one thing, two ends of the same house.

She never asked if it worked

She did this for twenty, maybe thirty years. I looked at old photos once, trying to see if her hairline had moved. I couldn't tell. Maybe the ginger held it in place. Maybe she just had the kind of hair that doesn't retreat. She never asked the question. "Doing it is enough," she'd say. "The rest isn't my business."

She wasn't chasing a result the way we think about results now — no before-and-after, no tracking, no measuring. She just believed that something fresh and alive pressed against the skin would wake up whatever was sleeping there. That was the whole theory. She applied the same logic to her face after a long day — not potions or creams, just a warm towel folded over her eyes for five minutes while she sat on the edge of the bed.

I looked it up once

Years after she died, I typed "ginger hair growth" into a search engine. Half the results said it worked. Half said it didn't. There were studies. There were forum threads. There were products with ginger extract in them, sold in little bottles for money. I read for maybe ten minutes and closed the tab. I didn't need to know. What I had was the image of her in the bathroom every Sunday, bending toward the mirror with a piece of ginger in her hand, and that was worth more than an answer. Some things in this house were done not because they had been proven to work, but because her mother had done them, and her mother before her, and the chain was more important than the proof.

I still have a piece of ginger in my bathroom. I don't use it every Sunday. But when I do, I cut it fresh, and I bend forward, and for a minute I'm seven years old again, standing on my toes behind her.
