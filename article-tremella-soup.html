The Pale Fungus That Made a Beauty of Every Woman in the Hutong

The first time I saw a soaked tremella in my grandmother's kitchen, I thought she was washing an old sponge. The dried thing was hard as a pebble — a single pale knot no bigger than a walnut, bought from the medicine shop for a few coins. But after four hours in cold water it had swollen into a cloud the size of both hands, translucent, slippery to the touch. She called it yin er (银耳) — silver ear, from the way the soaked pieces curled like soft white petals. She tore it into shreds the size of her thumbnail. I asked why so small, and she said, "The smaller they are, the more obediently they melt."

Cold water, never hot

Soaking tremella had rules — and the first one was cold water. I asked why she couldn't use hot water. She shook her head. "Hot water makes it seize up," she said. "The good part stays trapped inside." So the dried knot went into a wide ceramic bowl of cold tap water, and it sat there all afternoon. I'd sit at the table doing homework, glancing up now and then — the white thing growing larger, clearer, more like a living creature and less like a dried husk. Four hours, sometimes five. She never timed it. She just looked at it and knew.

Once it was soft, she picked it up with both hands and inspected it. The hard yellow nub at the base — the root end — had to be cut out with scissors. "That part is bitter," she said. Then came the tearing. She didn't use a knife. Fingers only, pulling the pale mass apart into shreds, the smaller the better. When I asked why she tore it by hand instead of chopping it, she said the edges torn by hand released more of the silk. I don't know if that's true. I only know she believed it.

The long wait

Cooking tremella was slow work, and slow work was the one kind my grandmother never rushed. She never looked at a clock. "It'll let you know when it's ready," she'd say. The pot went on a low flame — never high. "A fierce flame won't draw the gel out," she explained. "Only a gentle one will."

Halfway through, she'd lift the lid and stir — fast, almost violent, the spoon rattling against the sides of the pot, the liquid churning. I asked what she was doing. "Urging it," she said, as if the soup were a sleepy child that needed to be nudged awake. The rock sugar went in last — on this point she was strict. "Sugar that goes in too early blocks the gel. You have to wait until it's almost done." The goji berries came even later, just before the flame went off, a small handful scattered across the surface. "If you put them in too soon," she said, "the skins split and the whole pot turns sour." The goji berries she used came from the same tin she reached for every afternoon when she wanted something simple — just a handful of dried red berries in hot water.

The spoon test

My grandmother had one way to check if the soup was done, and it had nothing to do with a timer. She'd dip a spoon into the pot and lift it slowly. If the liquid ran off the spoon in a thin, fast stream, it wasn't ready. But if it clung — if it slid off slowly, reluctantly, pulling a single long thread between the spoon and the pot — then the flame went off. She never said the word "collagen." She didn't know it. She just said the soup had "come together."

Cooled to room temperature, it thickened even more. A spoonful lifted from the bowl would hold its shape for a moment before collapsing. She called it "drinkable silk." The women of the hutong, she told me, all simmered this — "for the face." When I asked if it was like the powder they put on their cheeks, she laughed. "No, no. That kind of glow washes off. This one comes from inside." I never saw my grandmother wear makeup. I never saw her look in a mirror for longer than a few seconds. But I remember her skin in her seventies — smooth, pale, almost translucent in the morning light. Maybe it was the soup. Maybe it was just the kind of skin you get from not rushing anything. The same kitchen that turned out bowls of tremella also simmered mung bean soup on summer afternoons — a different pot, a different answer to the same heat.

Years later, in a kitchen that smelled of nothing in particular, I bought a packet of dried tremella from a Chinese grocery store. The instructions on the back said to soak it for thirty minutes. I ignored them. I filled a bowl with cold water, dropped the pale knot in, and let it sit for four hours. When I tore it apart with my fingers, I thought of her hands — knuckles swollen, nails short, moving without hurry. The pot was stainless steel now, the stove electric. But when the lid came off and I stirred fast and heard the spoon rattle against the metal, the sound was the same. The sugar went in late. The goji berries went in last. When I lifted the spoon and watched the liquid pull a single long thread, I nearly called out to her. The first bowl tasted almost right. But the kitchen was quiet in a way hers never was, and I knew I had left one thing out — the thing that was never in the recipe.
