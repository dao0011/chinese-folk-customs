When the Cough Wouldn't Stop, Grandma Went to the Kitchen

I was seven, coughing through the night, when my grandmother disappeared into the dark kitchen. She came back with a steaming bowl of pear water. No explanation. Just a bowl, a spoon, and a lifetime of knowing what to do.

The night I couldn't stop coughing

I was seven or eight, in the middle of winter, coughing so hard the whole household woke up. My grandmother threw a coat over her shoulders and disappeared into the kitchen. Half an hour later she came back with a steaming bowl of pear water. One pear, a few pieces of rock sugar, water. I drank it. She sat on the edge of the bed and waited until I finished, then turned off the light and went back to sleep. She didn't say much the whole time. It took me decades to understand — it wasn't medicine. It was a lifetime of experience turned into care for her grandchild. Someone in the house was unwell, so the kitchen had to be put to work.

What was in the bowl

One snow pear cut into chunks, a few pieces of yellow rock sugar, water. Low heat, slow simmer, half an hour. That was it. My grandmother's generation didn't draw a line between "folk remedy" and "recipe" — there was food in the kitchen, someone in the house wasn't feeling right, so you used what the kitchen had. Not much sugar — one or two pieces was enough. The pear carried its own sweetness.

It's easy to overthink something this simple. A pear, some rock sugar, water, and half an hour of low heat. That's the whole list. No measurements. No brand of pear. My grandmother didn't weigh the sugar or set a timer. She watched the pot until the pear turned translucent and the water took on the faintest amber tint — then she poured it into a bowl and carried it to the bedside. The same kitchen logic that put ginger and dates in hot water after a cold meal also put a pear in a pot when a child couldn't stop coughing. Not because anyone had proven anything. Because someone before her had done it, and it had helped.

Why she never explained it

My grandmother almost never explained anything. She brought the pear water to the bedside the same way she brought dinner — as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. In her universe, a cough called for pear water the same way darkness called for a light switch. No manual required. This knowledge had traveled through who knows how many generations. Nobody had ever written down "pear cures cough" — they only knew: grandson is coughing, get a pear and yellow rock sugar, simmer a bowl of water.

This is the same silent transmission line that carried scallion white root tea through generations of kitchens. Nobody wrote down the recipe for scallion water either. Nobody needed to. When a cold was coming, you reached for the scallions. When a cough wouldn't settle, you reached for a pear. The knowledge lived in hands, not in books. My grandmother would have been confused by the question "why does pear water work?" — not because she didn't know, but because the question itself belonged to a different way of thinking. In her kitchen, you didn't ask why. You watched, you remembered, and when it was your turn, you did the same.

I made it for my own child

Years later, when my own child couldn't stop coughing, I walked into the kitchen and reached for a pear without thinking. Not a memory, not a search — my body moved on its own. My grandmother's hands had become my hands. The method: snow pear, skin on, a piece or two of yellow rock sugar, low heat, slow simmer, carry to the bedside. Thirty years later, the same late-night hour, the same motion. That's what passing something down really looks like — not taught, just remembered.

I didn't think about it. I didn't call my mother to ask how much sugar. I just walked into the kitchen, took a pear from the fruit bowl, and started cutting. The motion was already in my hands — thirty years of watching had done what no recipe could do. The pear turned translucent. The water took on that faint amber color. I carried the bowl to my child's bedside, sat down, and waited. The same silence. The same spoon. The same quiet certainty that the kitchen, when it's all you have, is enough.
