Hundred Strokes: The Bedtime Wooden-Comb Ritual

The last sound I heard every night as a child was not the television. It was not the radio. It was the soft, rhythmic whisper of a wooden comb passing through my grandmother's grey hair — shhh, shhh, shhh — slow and even, one stroke at a time. She sat on the edge of her bed in the dim light of the bedside lamp, her hair loosened from its daytime knot, pulling the wide-toothed comb from her forehead back toward the nape of her neck. One. Two. Three. She counted in her head — I know because sometimes her lips moved. One hundred strokes, every night, without fail. Not for beauty. Not for hair care. Just the last quiet task of the day, the thing that closed the door on the hours behind her.

The last sound of the day

The house had its own sequence of evening sounds, and the comb was always last. First came the clink of dishes being washed. Then the creak of the bathroom door. Then the click of the bedside lamp. And finally, when everything else was still, the comb. If you listened from the next room, you could track her progress — the strokes were slower toward the end, as if the counting itself was winding her down. By ninety, the rhythm had softened. By one hundred, the comb was set on the nightstand, the lamp clicked off, and the house was silent.

I asked her once, when I was maybe nine, why she did it. She looked surprised by the question — not offended, just genuinely unprepared to explain something so ordinary. "It untangles the day," she said after a moment. That was the whole answer. I waited for more, but she'd already picked up the comb and started counting.

Years later, I think I understand what she meant. The day leaves tangles — small frustrations, unfinished conversations, the residue of things that didn't go right. You can carry them into bed or you can work them out, one stroke at a time, before you close your eyes.

Why wood, and why one hundred

My grandmother was particular about the comb in a way she was not particular about most things. It had to be wood — boxwood if she could find it, peach wood if she couldn't. "Plastic makes the hair stand up," she'd say, meaning static. What she meant was that plastic combs made a faint crackling sound and left the ends of her hair floating away from her head, resistant and unruly. Wood was quiet. Wood was smooth. Wood had been cut from something living and carried a trace of that warmth into the hand.

The teeth had to be wide. Not the fine-toothed comb she used for parting and styling in the morning — that one was for daytime, for precision. The bedtime comb was different. Its teeth were spaced far enough apart that it couldn't snag, couldn't pull, couldn't turn a calming ritual into a small violence. "You're not untangling knots," she said. "You're smoothing what's already there."

And one hundred — a round number, a complete number, the kind of number that feels finished. She never counted aloud. Sometimes her lips moved. Sometimes she'd lose track around sixty and start over. The number mattered less than the rhythm. One hundred strokes — not one hundred and one, not ninety-nine — was simply the amount of time it took for her shoulders to drop and her breathing to slow and the day to feel properly closed. "One hundred is enough," she said. "Less than that and you're rushing. More than that and you're stalling."

What this does that scrolling doesn't

I think about this ritual every time I catch myself in bed at midnight, phone in hand, thumb moving endlessly upward through a feed that never ends. The contrast is almost too neat: a wooden comb versus a glass screen, a fixed count versus infinite scroll, a single sensation versus a flood of information. My grandmother's hundred strokes took three to five minutes. That's roughly the length of three short videos, or one spiral through a comment section, or the time it takes to check four apps and find nothing new in any of them.

The difference isn't just the absence of a screen. It's the direction of attention. Scrolling pulls you outward — into other people's lives, other people's opinions, a world that is always demanding a reaction. Combing pulls you inward — the sensation is on your own scalp, the rhythm is your own rhythm, the counting is your own quiet voice. Nothing is being asked of you. Nothing is being sold to you. Your hands are doing something simple and repetitive, and your mind, given nothing else to do, begins to settle.

This was the rhythm my grandmother lived by long before anyone talked about sleep hygiene or blue light or winding down. She didn't need studies to tell her that the body needs a transition between waking and sleeping. She had the comb. She had the count. She had her mother's example and her grandmother's before that — a line of women who understood that a post-meal walk after dinner and a twenty-minute midday pause after lunch were all parts of the same idea: the body doesn't switch off on command. You have to coax it, gently, with small rituals repeated until they become instinct.

Your own count

There is nothing complicated about this, and that's the point:

  • The comb: A wide-toothed wooden comb — boxwood, peach wood, sandalwood, whatever you can find. Not plastic. Not metal. Not fine-toothed. Something that glides without catching.
  • The time: Right before sleep, after everything else is done. Sit on the edge of the bed. Let your hair down. The lamp can be on or off — whatever feels right.
  • The stroke: Start at the hairline above your forehead. Draw the comb back over your scalp toward the back of your neck. Light pressure — you're not scrubbing, you're smoothing. The teeth should touch the scalp but not dig in.
  • The count: Fifty if you're tired. One hundred if you're not. Don't rush. If you lose count, start over or don't — the number is the least important part of this. What matters is the time you gave yourself before sleep.
