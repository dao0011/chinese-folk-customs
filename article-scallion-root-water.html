Scallion White Root Water: The First Thing My Grandmother Reached For

In my grandmother's house, the first sign of a chill — a sneeze at breakfast, a complaint of cold shoulders, a child saying "I don't feel good" — triggered a predictable kitchen response. She'd go to the basket of green onions on the counter, cut off the white root ends with a decisive snap of her knife, and drop them into a small pot of water. Fifteen minutes later, a steaming, salty, slightly oniony liquid would appear in a bowl, placed in front of whoever had sniffled first, with the same three words every time: "Drink this. Then go sleep."

Scallion white root water — cong bai shui (葱白水) — is not a tea, not a soup, not a medicine. It's a kitchen scrap transformed into an act of care. The white part of a green onion — the section just above the roots, pale and crisp — is the part she used. Not the green tops, not the very bottom with the little root hairs, but the thick, white, crunchy segment in between. That part, she explained once when I was old enough to ask, was what the kitchen had always used when someone needed warming up.

The whole process took maybe twenty minutes. She'd rinse the white root sections, slice them lengthwise into thin strips, drop them into a pot of cold water, add a generous pinch of salt — she said salt helped the warmth get deeper — and bring it to a boil. Then she'd lower the heat and let it simmer for ten minutes while the house filled with a clean, pungent, earthy smell that was unmistakably onion but not quite like anything you'd recognize from cooking dinner. She'd pour the liquid into a bowl through a strainer, discarding the cooked onion strips, and hand the bowl over with the instruction: drink it while it's hot, then get under a blanket. Usually within an hour, the person who had been shivering would be sweating and falling asleep.

Where this habit comes from

Scallion white root appears in Chinese folk practices across many regions, but its use as a warmth-inducing kitchen drink is especially common in central and southern China, where the winters are damp rather than dry, and where a "cold feeling" was often described as something that had to be physically driven out of the body. The folk logic — as my grandmother explained it to me — was simple: scallion white root has a strong, sharp taste that makes you sweat, and sweating is what your body does naturally when it has finished fighting off the cold.

My grandfather, who recited folk sayings the way other people recited prayers, had one for this too: "Hot water and a good sweat are cheaper than any doctor." He said it often enough that it became a household joke — but nobody in the house ever doubted the underlying idea. When you felt cold and heavy, you drank something hot, you wrapped yourself in blankets, and you let your body do whatever it needed to do. The scallion root water was just the vehicle for that heat.

For an even stronger warming drink, my grandmother would sometimes combine the white onion root with fresh ginger slices in the same pot — the two flavors blended into something that tasted like savory, peppery broth without any meat in it. That combination, she said, was for the days when the chill felt like it was in the bones, not just on the skin.

What you'll need

  • 3 to 5 green onions (scallions) — fresh, with firm white roots attached
  • A small pot — just big enough to hold 2 cups of water
  • Salt — about half a teaspoon
  • A strainer
  • A bowl or mug
  • Optional: 2 to 3 slices of fresh ginger, for a stronger warming effect

Steps

  1. Wash the green onions and cut off the white root section — about the bottom 2 inches of each onion, including the pale part just above the root base. Discard the green tops (save them for cooking if you like).
  2. Slice the white root sections lengthwise into thin strips. This helps the flavor release faster.
  3. Put the sliced white roots into a small pot with 2 cups of cold water. Add half a teaspoon of salt.
  4. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, then reduce the heat to low and simmer for 10 minutes. The liquid will turn a pale, cloudy color and the kitchen will fill with a clean onion scent.
  5. Strain the liquid into a bowl, discarding the cooked onion strips.
  6. Drink the liquid hot — as hot as you can comfortably sip. The goal is to feel warmth spreading from the inside out.
  7. Immediately after drinking, get under a blanket, wear warm socks, and rest. If you start to sweat lightly, that's the kitchen sign that the scallion water did what it was supposed to do.

How different households make it

The basic version — white onion root, water, salt — is the standard. But variations exist across the kitchens I grew up around:

  • With ginger — 2 or 3 slices of fresh ginger simmered alongside the scallion root for a stronger, spicier heat.
  • With brown sugar — a teaspoon of Chinese brown sugar (or dark muscovado) stirred in at the end, turning the drink from savory to sweet-and-salty. This version was often given to children.
  • With black pepper — a pinch of ground black pepper added to the bowl just before drinking, for an extra layer of warmth.
  • Double-strength — using more scallion roots (6 to 8) and half the water, for a concentrated version that was drunk in smaller sips over a longer period.

None of these is the "right" version. Each kitchen made it the way the household liked it. The only rule my grandmother ever acknowledged was that it had to be drunk hot and followed by rest.

Comfort and safety

Scallion root water is a food-based kitchen drink, not a supplement or medication. The main safety consideration is temperature — very hot liquids can burn the mouth and throat. Let the drink cool just enough to sip comfortably before drinking. If the onion flavor feels too strong, use fewer scallion roots or add more water next time.

Some people find that scallions and onions cause mild stomach irritation or heartburn. If that describes you, skip this habit entirely. The practice is meant to feel comforting, not like an obligation.

The folk logic behind this drink — heat a person up, induce mild sweating, then let them rest — is a commonsensical approach to feeling cold and uncomfortable. It is not a treatment for any medical condition. If you have a fever above 101°F (38.3°C), difficulty breathing, severe body aches, or symptoms that persist beyond a day, you should consult a healthcare professional. This kitchen habit belongs at the very beginning of a chill, when someone just feels a little off and wants to feel warm — not as a substitute for medical care when symptoms are serious.

Who should check with a professional first

Anyone managing high blood pressure should be aware that this drink contains added salt. If you are on a sodium-restricted diet, reduce or omit the salt in the recipe, or skip the drink altogether and rely on plain hot water and rest instead. Pregnant women and young children should also check with a healthcare professional before using this or similar warming folk drinks, not because scallions are known to cause harm — they're just a common vegetable — but because individual tolerance varies and caution is always appropriate for vulnerable groups.

The Ben Cao Gang Mu — a comprehensive sixteenth-century text by Li Shizhen that compiled knowledge about food and household ingredients — describes scallion white root as a warming substance used in folk kitchens when someone felt cold, heavy, or unwell. I include this reference as historical context, not as validation. The entry tells us what people in Ming Dynasty China thought about a common kitchen ingredient. That's interesting history, not an instruction.

If you live outside China

Green onions — scallions, spring onions — are available in every grocery store in the world, usually sold in bunches of five or six. Any variety will work for this drink as long as the white root section is thick and firm. Avoid onions that are wilted, slimy, or yellowing — freshness matters here more than it does in cooking, because the drink's flavor comes entirely from the raw onion essence.

If you can't eat onions due to allergy, intolerance, or personal preference, the principle behind this habit is still available to you: a very hot drink, consumed slowly under a blanket, followed by rest. Plain hot water with a pinch of salt, ginger tea without the scallion, or even a strong cup of black tea can serve the same purpose — to warm you from inside on a day when you feel cold and tired. The scallion root is just the kitchen's most direct way of doing that.
