Lotus Root Water: The Pale Summer Sip

On summer market days, lotus root came home with damp soil still caught between the joints. My grandmother rinsed it at the sink, cut a few pale slices from one section, and set them in a small pot of water at the side of the stove. No one announced a recipe. The pot simply stayed there, the way boiled water or leftover tea stayed there, waiting for someone to pour a cup.

A root from the wet market

Lotus root was not bought only for this drink. It was a market vegetable first: scrubbed clean, sliced for a quick stir-fry, tucked into soup, or saved for a dish with pork ribs when the house had more people to feed. The water came from the extra piece on the board, often an end section too small to become a full dish.

She washed the mud from the holes with the tip of a chopstick, trimmed away the dark spots, and cut the root crosswise. The slices made their own pattern in the pot: white flesh, round windows, cloudy edges where the starch met the water.

The pot at the side of the stove

The household version was loose. Slices went into water. The pot warmed while other work happened around it. If she passed the stove, my grandmother lifted the lid and pressed the floating pieces down with chopsticks. Sometimes a little rock sugar went in. Often nothing did.

It belonged to the same summer shelf as mung bean soup and winter melon tea, but it asked for less attention than either of them. Mung beans had to split. Winter melon had to give up its liquid. Lotus root only had to sit in water long enough for the water to turn faintly cloudy.

Not a dish, not quite tea

In English it is tempting to call this tea, but no tea leaves are involved. It is closer to vegetable water, the kind of thing a kitchen makes when an ingredient is already present and the stove is already in use. The taste is light. The color is light. The work is light.

Compared with sour plum drink, lotus root water had no shopfront feeling. No smoked plums, no osmanthus, no dark jar cooling in the corner. It was kitchen-side and plain, poured into whatever cup was closest.

The slices left behind

After the water was poured, the lotus root slices did not become waste. They stayed in the pot for the next cup, or they were fished out with chopsticks and eaten at the counter. This part matters. The habit was not separate from household thrift. It used the same thinking that saved rice water instead of sending it straight down the drain.

By evening, the pot was usually empty. The slices looked tired by then, less crisp than when they were cut, but still edible. Someone ate them because they were there.

What this page records

This is not a formal preparation. It is a small kitchen behavior: lotus root bought for cooking, a few slices warmed in water, cups poured between other tasks. That is the record here.
