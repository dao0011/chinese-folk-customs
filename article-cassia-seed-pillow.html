Cassia Seed Pillow: A Seed Pillow from an Old Chinese Bedroom

My grandmother kept one pillow apart from the others in summer. It was smaller, flatter, and firm enough to hold its shape when she lifted it from the bed. Inside the cotton cover were cassia seeds, jue ming zi, dark and glossy, making a dry rustle whenever she turned the pillow over.

The pillow that rustled

The sound came before the object. A soft shh-shh when she shook the pillow flat, then a quieter settling as the seeds found their place inside the cloth. She did this at the edge of the bed after the mosquito net was lowered. The bamboo mat was already wiped down. The fan sat within reach. The seed pillow went where the cotton pillow had been.

The cover was made from plain cloth, not new cloth. The same box held scraps for coarse salt warm packs, drawer liners, and small kitchen ties. A row of cloth buttons kept the seeds in. When I pressed the pillow with my hand, it did not collapse. My grandmother took it back, patted the surface twice, and said, "This one is for hot nights. Soft pillows make the head restless."

Summer bedroom logic

The pillow belonged to the room around it. Summer bedding in her house was arranged by touch: bamboo below, thin cotton above, no heavy quilt, open window, mosquito net tied up before dusk and lowered after dinner. The seed pillow was not decorative. It sat beside other objects chosen because air could pass around them or heat did not gather quickly in them.

That same logic explains the bamboo wife, the hollow woven cylinder once kept in southern bedrooms for humid nights. It also explains why quilts were moved into the sun during bedding airing, then beaten and folded before storage. The house changed with the weather. No announcement was made. Objects moved from shelf to bed, from bed back to shelf.

What she did with the seeds

Once in a while, my grandmother opened the pillow and poured the seeds into a large enamel bowl. She checked them with her fingers, looking for dampness. If the day was clear, she spread them on newspaper near the window, not in the middle of the yard where dust would blow across them. Later she tipped the paper into a funnel shape and slid the seeds back into the cover.

The cloth wore out before the filling did. I remember one cover with blue flowers, another with a faded check pattern. The seeds stayed. She would hold a handful above the bowl, let them fall back through her fingers, and say, "Old seeds lie smoother." Then the opening was closed again and the pillow returned to the bed.

The sayings around it

People in the family had explanations for the pillow. Some said cassia seed was for the eyes. Some said it was for a heavy head in summer. My grandmother kept the sayings short. "For hot nights," she said once. Another time, when I asked why the pillow was so hard, she said, "It keeps you from turning all over the bed."

She did not argue for it. She used it. At night she adjusted the seed pillow with both hands, lowered her head onto the firm middle, and left the softer pillows stacked near the wall. In the morning, she stood it on its side for a while before making the bed.

Stored after summer

When the nights cooled, the seed pillow disappeared from the bed. It went into a cloth bag at the top of the wardrobe, above folded sheets and winter pillowcases. The bamboo mat was rolled. The mosquito net was washed and put away. The bed became soft again.

I did not like that pillow as a child. It was too firm, and the seeds moved when I wanted stillness. My grandmother did not try to give me one. She only said, "You will know when a hard pillow makes sense." Then she put the cover back on, shook the seeds level, and placed it on her side of the bed.

A quick word

This article records a household object for cultural interest only. Keep seed-filled pillows dry and clean; avoid using them around small children, pets, allergies, mold, or chewing risks.
