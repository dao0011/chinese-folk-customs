Black Sesame Walnut Paste: The Dark Sweet Bowl

My grandmother's stone mortar lived on the bottom shelf of the kitchen cabinet. I knew black sesame walnut paste was coming when she pulled it out with both hands and set it on the table with a dull thud. A bowl of black sesame seeds, a few walnuts, a small paper bag of rock sugar. Nothing looked special yet. Then the pan warmed, the seeds darkened, and the kitchen took on that roasted smell that made everyone look up from what they were doing.

The sound of the mortar

The mortar had its own sound. Not loud, but steady: stone against seed, then a softer scrape once the walnuts began to give up their oil. My grandmother sat with the mortar braced against the table edge and moved the pestle in a slow circle. She did not talk much while grinding. She watched the paste instead, stopping now and then to scrape the sides back down.

I asked once why she did not use the grinder we kept for spices. She said the machine made it too fast and too hot. Then she went back to the mortar. That was usually how her kitchen explanations ended: one sentence, then the work continued. The grinding belonged to the bowl, the way stirring plain rice congee belonged to the morning pot.

A bowl for grey days

She did not make this paste often. It was not an everyday food like congee or ginger tea. It came out when the sky stayed grey, when laundry would not dry, or when there were walnuts in the cupboard that needed using. The bowl was sweet, dark, and heavy enough to slow the hand holding the spoon.

The paste arrived warm, dense enough that the spoon left a trail. A few walnut pieces might sit on top. Sometimes she scattered white sesame over the black surface because she liked the contrast. I remember the first spoonful more for texture than taste: thick, grainy, sweet at the edge, with the walnut oil rounding out the sesame. No one ate it standing up. You sat down for this bowl.

The hair thing

The hair saying belonged to the folklore around this food, and she did say it. "Black sesame keeps the hair dark," she told me once, tapping her own silver bun with the pestle still in her other hand. Then she laughed at herself and kept grinding. In the kitchen, the saying mattered less than the bowl on the table.

The practical part was simpler. Black sesame and walnuts kept well in the pantry. They could be toasted, ground, sweetened lightly, and turned into something that felt more substantial than tea but less formal than dessert. That was enough reason to keep a mortar on the bottom shelf.

The dark bowl

I do not make it as often as she did. When I do, I understand why she kept the old mortar even after faster tools entered the kitchen. Some foods are partly made by waiting beside them. Black sesame walnut paste sits somewhere between food and sweet: thicker than longan red date tea, less plain than congee, quieter than festival desserts. Toast, grind, warm, spoon into a bowl. That is the part I remember.

A quick word

This article records a traditional food custom for cultural interest only. Black sesame and walnuts are common allergens for some people.
