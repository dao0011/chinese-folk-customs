The Dark Amber Drink That Cooled a Beijing Summer

In July, the Beijing hutong where my grandmother lived got so hot the asphalt softened underfoot. The first thing she did each morning was not make breakfast. It was walk into the kitchen and check on the jar of suan mei tang (酸梅汤) — the sour plum drink she had simmered the night before and left to cool, now chilling on the door of the fridge. Every household in the hutong kept its own jar. When neighbours dropped by, the first question was never "have you eaten?" but "have you simmered your sour plum yet?" My grandfather, coming back from fixing someone's bicycle, wouldn't bother with plain water — he'd pour a glass straight from the jar and drink it standing up, head tilted back. From June to August that jar was almost never empty: finish one pot, simmer the next. The enamel pot that held it went unwashed for the whole season, the bottom stained a dark brown ring that my grandmother refused to scrub. "That's not dirt," she'd say. "That's the pot's memory."

Summer in a Beijing hutong

The hutong had its own summer logic. By mid-morning the narrow lane between the grey brick walls would hold the heat like an oven, and the only air that moved came from hand-held palm fans. Nobody cooked a big lunch in July. The kitchen was the hottest room in the courtyard, and the stove was lit as little as possible. What people wanted, in that heat, was something cold and sour and dark — something that made the mouth pucker and the back of the jaw ache pleasantly, something that felt, in my grandmother's word, "jie shu" (解暑) — "to undo the summer heat." She would never have called it a health claim. It was just what the body wanted in July, the way it wanted congee in winter. The sour plum drink was the answer the hutong had agreed on, the way other regions agreed on mung bean soup or winter melon tea.

The jar lived on the fridge door because that was the coldest spot in the house. Before we had a fridge, my grandmother kept it in a clay basin of well water, wrapped in a damp cloth so the evaporation would pull the heat out. Either way, by afternoon the drink was cold enough to make the glass fog when you poured it. That first sip — sour, smoky, faintly sweet, with a long dark finish — is the taste I most associate with being seven years old and overheated and suddenly, completely fine.

Seven things from the medicine shop

The ingredients did not come from a grocery store. They came from the traditional medicine shop at the mouth of the hutong. My grandmother walked in the way you walk into your own kitchen. "Lao Zhang, the usual," she'd say, and the man behind the counter would weigh out seven things, each folded into its own square of yellow paper: smoked dark plums (wu mei, 乌梅), 80 grams, black and glossy, smelling of woodsmoke; dried hawthorn berries (shan zha, 山楂), 80 grams, whole and wrinkled around their pits; aged tangerine peel (chen pi, 陈皮), 10 grams, three years old, releasing a deep warm smell when you bent it; liquorice root (gan cao, 甘草), 10 grams, sliced thin, with a slow sweet aftertaste when you bit a piece; dried mulberries (sang shen, 桑葚), 15 grams, so dark they were almost purple and sticky to the touch; dried roselle (luo shen hua, 洛神花), 10 grams, a deep magenta that stung the inside of your mouth raw if you tasted one plain. Then a slab of yellow rock sugar, weighed separately. The osmanthus flowers came last, kept in a small tin at home.

The roselle, she told me more than once, was the one in charge of the colour. "Add more and it goes red. But not too much, or the sourness buries the smoke of the plum." She treated those seven ingredients the way a painter treats a palette — the same seven things, every summer, and yet no two pots ever came out exactly alike, because the plums were smokier some years, the hawthorn tarter, the peel older.

The recipe itself, she said, had once belonged to the medicine shop. In old texts it was called wu mei yin (乌梅饮) — smoked plum drink — and it was catalogued as a summer preparation for clearing heat. Somewhere over the centuries it walked out of the pharmacy and into the kitchen, stopped being a preparation and became a drink, and never went back. The chrysanthemum flowers she steeped in autumn had made the same journey — both began on a herbalist's shelf and ended up in a household jar, the line between "from the medicine shop" and "from the kitchen" worn smooth by a thousand summers.

The pot that never left the stove

My grandmother never wrote the method down, but I can recite every step from watching her. First: the seven dried things go into the enamel pot, and the water comes up three fingers above them. No soaking, no scrubbing. "Soak them too long and you wash the colour and the goodness right out," she'd say — a quick rinse of the surface dust under the tap was all they ever got. Second: bring it to a hard boil, then drop to a low simmer and leave it for forty minutes. Third: add the yellow rock sugar. It had to be the yellow kind, never the white. "Yellow rock sugar is moistening," she'd say. "White rock sugar is only sweet." Two or three more minutes of simmering, until the sugar dissolved, then off the heat.

Fourth — and this was the closest thing she had to a stern commandment — wait for the drink to cool before you scatter in the osmanthus. "Remember this if you remember nothing else," she said. "If the liquid is still hot when you add the flowers, the whole pot turns bitter." I have never forgotten the way she said it, leaning over the pot with the tin of osmanthus in one hand, waiting for the steam to slow. Fifth: strain it through a cloth. The strained-out dregs she never threw away. She'd cover them with fresh water and simmer a second pot — thinner in flavour, but good enough for a hot afternoon. Only then did the dark amber liquid go into the glass jar and into the fridge.

She measured none of it. In her whole life she never wrote down a recipe; her hand was the scale, the same instinctive logic by which she made a pot of ginger tea without measuring the water or counting the slices. The plums knew how dark to make the pot. The sugar knew how sweet. She just watched and tasted and trusted the seven ingredients to do what they had always done.

I made it three thousand miles from the hutong

Years later, in a Chinatown an ocean away, I gathered the seven things one by one from a herbalist who had to order half of them. It took three months. When I finally set the pot on the stove and the house filled with the smoked sourness of the dark plums and the old warm smell of the tangerine peel, I half-turned, expecting my grandmother to be standing behind me.

Straining out the dregs, I heard her voice: don't throw them away, simmer another pot. Scattering the osmanthus, my hand hesitated — the liquid was still warm, and I pulled the bowl aside and waited, afraid of bittering the whole batch. The first sip nearly undid me. The colour was right. The taste was close. But the hutong was gone, and my grandmother was gone, and the enamel pot with the brown ring was gone, and old Lao Zhang's "the usual" was gone. It was not really a drink. It was July, and Beijing, and a place that no longer exists, carried in a glass.

I kept the ingredient list, the only part of her that I can pass on: smoked dark plums 80 g, dried hawthorn 80 g, aged tangerine peel 10 g, liquorice root 10 g, dried mulberries 15 g, dried roselle 10 g, yellow rock sugar to taste, water to cover by three fingers. Simmer forty minutes. Cool. Scatter osmanthus. Strain. Chill overnight.
