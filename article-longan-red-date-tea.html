The Red Water They Called Goddess Tea

My grandmother had a small milk pot that was never used for milk. It was for one thing only: dried longan and red dates, simmered together until the water turned a deep reddish brown. Every time someone in the hutong gave birth, she picked up that pot and walked it straight to their door. Not congee, not eggs — a pot of red water. The dried longan were dark brown and shriveled, each one a tight fist of caramel sweetness. The red dates had to be split open by hand, their pits pulled out and the flesh torn so the water could get inside. That pot sat on her stove from one end of the year to the other, because someone in the hutong was always having a baby, always recovering, always pale.

The pot that never cooled

That little pot had a permanent spot on the back burner. It was small — maybe a liter — and the inside was stained a permanent reddish-brown from years of the same brew. My grandmother never scrubbed that stain. She said it was seasoned, like a wok. The longan came from the dried-goods drawer in a paper bag that rustled when you reached in. The red dates were the big ones — hong zao, not the small black jujubes — and she'd sit at the kitchen table and tear them open one by one, flicking the pits into a bowl. "If you leave the pit in, the water stays thin," she said. "The good part can't get out."

She never measured. A handful of longan, a handful of dates, the pot filled with cold water. Bring it to a boil, then push the flame down until the bubbles were barely breaking the surface. The kitchen would fill with a smell that was half caramel and half something darker — like molasses, but cleaner. In winter, the steam from that pot was the warmest thing in the house. The pot never really went cold. Someone would finish a bowl, and she'd add more water to stretch it another round. "Drink it all," she'd say. "The dates too."

They called it Goddess Tea

In the hutong, this brew had a name louder than anything on a medicine-shop shelf. They called it Goddess Tea — nv shen cha. No brand registered it. No one owned it. It was just what three generations of women had called it, and the name stuck because the name was right. Girls drank it when their periods started. New brides drank it in the month after childbirth. Even my grandmother, well into her seventies, poured herself a bowl on winter nights when her hands and feet wouldn't warm up. "Women lose blood their whole lives," she said. "It's never too late to put some back."

In the hutong, the math was simple: a bottle of perfume cost a month's wages and faded by noon. A pot of red water cost a handful of dried fruit and stayed warm all day. The women around her didn't need the comparison spelled out. They just nodded when she appeared at the door with the pot. Nobody in that alleyway ever called ginger tea a goddess — ginger was what you reached for when your stomach was cold or your feet wouldn't warm. Good, solid, necessary. But red water was for blood. That was women's territory, and they named it accordingly.

A bowl for every woman who bled

She started with cold water — always cold, she said hot water trapped something inside the fruit that should come out slow. The pot went on high until it boiled, then she'd skim the foam off the top with a spoon and turn the flame down to the lowest notch. When the dates had gone soft and swollen, the longan had puffed up to twice their dried size, and the water was the color of strong black tea with a red undertone — the color of blood returning to skin that had been too pale.

If the person she was bringing it to had cold hands, she'd add two slices of ginger. If they were nursing, she'd leave it out — ginger could dry up the milk, she said. She knew every woman in the hutong by name and by condition. Mrs. Zhang's daughter-in-law, third floor, first baby, bleeding too long. Mrs. Li's youngest, second floor, just got her first period at thirteen. Mrs. Wang, first floor, seventy years old, winter was hard on her. One pot, one alleyway, one grandmother with a towel wrapped around the handle so she wouldn't burn her hands. She never took anything in return. "Women help women," she said. "That's how it works."

The color of blood returning

Years later, in a kitchen six thousand miles from that hutong, I bought dried longan from a Chinese grocery store and red dates from a Korean supermarket. The dates were a different variety — smaller, lighter in color — but when I tore them open and dropped them into the pot with the longan and set it to boil, the water still turned that same deep red. I stood over the stove and watched the color bloom, and the smell hit me before the taste did — the caramel, the molasses, the faint smokiness of dried fruit that had been simmering long enough to give up everything it had.

I took the first sip and almost cried. Not because of the taste — the taste was fine, close enough. It was the color. It was my grandmother walking down that alleyway with the pot in her hands, towel wrapped around the handle, steam rising past her face. It was every woman in the hutong who had ever lifted a bowl of that red water to her lips and smiled. Goji berries are red too — my grandmother's afternoon cup, quiet and solo, a handful of berries in hot water while she stared out the window. But nobody ever carried a pot of goji tea through the hutong. The red water was different. It was never just for one person.
