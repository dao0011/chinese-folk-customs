Turning Your Back to the Sun: A Midsummer Folk Ritual

During the hottest part of summer, my grandmother carried a low wooden stool into the courtyard and sat with her back to the morning sun for a short while. The neighbors did the same.

The east wall before breakfast

In late July, my grandmother carried a low wooden stool into the courtyard before breakfast. She placed it against the east wall, draped a thin towel over her shoulders, and turned her back to the rising sun. The stool was light enough to carry with one hand, but she set it down carefully as if the exact place mattered.

By the time I came downstairs, two other women had taken their own spots nearby. Nobody had arranged a meeting. They were simply following the same part of the day, each person quiet on her own patch of light.

Morning light, not midday heat

My grandmother was strict about the hour. The sun had to be low enough to feel warm through a cotton shirt, never harsh on the face or neck. If the paving stones were already hot, she folded the towel and went back inside without argument.

She stayed only until her back felt warm. Then she carried the stool to the shaded doorway, drank water, and checked whether the breakfast pot needed more time. The practice belonged to the season, but it was never a contest with the weather.

A courtyard habit remembered

She described the custom as something her mother had done during sanfu tian, the hottest stretch of the summer calendar. Other families used a balcony, a roof terrace, or the strip of sunlight beside a wall. The setting changed, but the small stool and the morning timing stayed familiar.

This is a cultural record, not a health prescription. What I remember most is the way the courtyard shared the light without turning the moment into a performance. When the sun moved past the wall, everyone stood, carried their stools inside, and returned to the ordinary work of the day.
