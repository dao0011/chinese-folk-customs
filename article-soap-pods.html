Soap Pods by the Kitchen Sink — A Chinese Household Tradition

Before shampoo bottles appeared on every shelf, my grandmother's kitchen had a bundle of dried soap pods hanging from a nail next to the sink. They were long and dark brown — some curved like a sickle, some nearly straight, each one the length of her hand, the pods of the honey locust tree. She called them zao jiao — "soap horn" — and they had been used for washing in Chinese households long enough that no one could tell you when the habit started. She'd break off a section, about two knuckles long, steep it in a bowl of hot water for ten minutes, and the water would turn faintly amber and slick to the touch. Rub it between your palms and it lathered — not the towering foam of a plastic bottle, but a fine, dense, creamy lather that cleaned everything it touched. The same pod served three purposes in three different corners of the house: hair by the basin, dishes by the sink, hands at the door — the way warm water from the kettle served every corner of the morning. One plant, one gesture, repeated in quiet variations for decades.

Not a fruit, not a soap — a pod from a thorn tree

Before I describe how she used them, let me say what soap pods actually are, because they're easy to confuse with things they're not. The honey locust — Gleditsia sinensis — is a tree native to China, covered in formidable branched thorns and producing long, flat, dark-brown seed pods that hang from the branches in autumn. The Chinese name zao jiao (皂角) translates literally as "soap horn" — zao for soap, jiao for the horn-like shape of the dried pod. When you see one hanging from a branch, the name makes perfect sense. It looks like a long, curved, dark horn — not at all like a berry or a fruit you'd want to eat.

The pods contain a high concentration of saponin, the same natural surfactant found in soapberries and soapwort and a handful of other plants around the world. When saponin meets water and friction, it creates foam. The chemistry is the same as modern detergent — a molecule with one end that grabs water and another end that grabs oil — but the engineering was done by a tree with thorns, over millions of years, without human intervention. Heat releases the saponin from the dried pod faster than cold water does, which is why my grandmother always started with hot water from the kettle.

The Bencao Gangmu (Compendium of Materia Medica), compiled by Li Shizhen in the sixteenth century, records zao jiao as a washing agent and notes its long history of household use. But by the Ming dynasty, people had already been using soap pods for so long that the herbalists couldn't trace a point of origin. Some habits don't have an inventor. They emerge from the overlap between what grows nearby and what people need, and soap pods are one of those — a tree people walked past every day, whose pods someone eventually discovered would foam in water. After that, the knowledge traveled sideways through kitchens and villages, mother to daughter, neighbor to neighbor, without ever passing through a book.

How she used them

My grandmother's soap pod routine was divided into three stations — three different purposes served by the same bundle hanging from the same nail. Each had its own rhythm and its own geography within the house.

Hair washing, once a week. On Sunday evenings, she'd take a whole dried pod from the bundle and break it into three or four sections with her hands — the pod was brittle when dry and snapped cleanly, like a dry twig. She'd drop the pieces into a basin and pour hot water from the kettle over them. Ten minutes later, the water had turned amber and felt slightly slippery when you dipped your fingers in. She'd swish her hand through it — a fine, dense foam would gather at the surface — then lean over the basin and work the soap pod water through her hair, from scalp to ends. She'd massage with her fingertips — not a modern scalp massage, just the practical motion of making sure the water reached everywhere — then rinse with plain water from the tap. No second product. No conditioner. Just zao jiao water and a wooden comb afterward. Her hair dried soft and clean, with no scent at all — just the smell of hair that had been washed and rinsed and left to dry in the air.

Dish washing, every day. The same pod sections, after the hair wash, weren't finished. She'd scoop them from the basin, drop them into the dish tub, and add more hot water from the kettle. A quick swish, and the water went faintly sudsy — not the towering suds of bottled detergent, but a fine, quiet lather that cut through cooking oil and left bowls and chopsticks clean. She'd wash, rinse under the tap, set everything on the wooden rack to dry. Then the pod pieces went back onto a small saucer on the windowsill — not thrown out, because they still had saponin left to give.

Hand washing, whenever she came in from outside. There was always a small dish on the bathroom shelf — just a shallow ceramic saucer with a piece or two of soap pod sitting in a little cold water. When she came back from the market or the vegetable patch, she'd pick up a piece, rub it between her wet palms for ten seconds until a light lather built up, then rinse and set it back in the saucer. The same piece would last another three or four hand-washes before it was spent. A soap pod gives its saponin slowly — not all at once, the way liquid soap pumps out in a predetermined dose, but gradually, across many uses. It's the kind of thing you only notice if you're paying attention to how much you use and how much is left — which my grandmother always was, not out of frugality but because paying attention to things was how she moved through the world.

The bundle that never ran out

The rhythm of the bundle was cyclical. A whole dried pod, hard and dark, came off the nail. A section was broken off and steeped. After the first use, the pod sections softened and darkened further — the way a dried shiitake darkens after soaking. She'd use them across all three stations over the course of several days — hair on Sunday, dishes on Monday and Tuesday, hands throughout. Then she'd spread them on the windowsill to dry. By Wednesday they'd be hard again, though slightly paler and rougher than before. Back into rotation. A single batch of pod sections might last two or three weeks before she said they were "tired" — at which point they'd turned a dull gray-brown and produced almost no lather at all. Only then did they go into the compost.

Every autumn, the honey locust trees around the village dropped their pods, and someone in the family — my grandmother, an aunt, one of the older cousins — gathered a basketful. They'd spread them on a bamboo tray to dry in the sun for several days, then bundle them with twine and hang them in a dry corner of the kitchen. One autumn harvest lasted the entire year. There's something quietly startling, in a world of monthly restocks and next-day delivery, about a cleaning product that has a harvest season and an annual rhythm. You couldn't run out of soap pods in August because August wasn't when they appeared. You used what you had, and you waited. The tree decided the schedule; the household adapted to it.

Variations I heard about

My grandmother's method — break, steep in hot water, use, dry, reuse — was one of several regional approaches. When I got older and talked to people from other parts of China, I learned that the soap pod tradition had as many variations as there were kitchens with a nail by the sink.

Some families boiled the pods. A ten-minute boil extracted more saponin faster and produced a darker, stronger liquid that could be strained into a bottle and used like liquid soap for a week or more. This was common in colder regions where the saponin didn't release as readily in warm water and where boiled water was always on hand anyway.

Some families mashed the pod sections with the flat side of a cleaver before steeping — a quick crush to expose more surface area to the water. Faster, foamier, but the pods broke down sooner and couldn't be reused as many times. A tradeoff between speed and thrift.

Some families used soap pods for laundry — whole garments soaked in a large basin of soap pod water, then beaten against a flat stone by the river or scrubbed on a washboard. This was the heaviest use, the one that consumed the most pods, and families that did laundry this way typically gathered twice as many pods each autumn as families that only used them for hair and dishes.

In some regions, soap pods were combined with rice water — the milky water left from washing rice — for an especially effective hair wash. The two household byproducts together made a wash that left hair smoother than either one alone. My grandmother sometimes did this, but not always. It depended on whether she'd washed rice at the right moment for the timing to line up. She didn't plan these combinations in advance. They happened or they didn't. The household economy was flexible that way.

In herbal shops, you could sometimes find zao jiao ground into a fine brown powder, sold in paper packets, to be mixed with water into a paste for washing. My grandmother considered this a needless refinement. "Why pay someone to grind it when the whole pod works fine?" she'd say. But she acknowledged that the powder was convenient for travel — not that she traveled much.

The smell of clean without perfume

If you've never smelled soap-pod water, the closest I can come is this: wet wood, a hint of something faintly herbal, and not much else. It doesn't smell like flowers. It doesn't smell like citrus or pine or any of the scents that modern advertising has taught us to associate with "clean." It smells like a tree after rain — earthy, neutral, barely there.

This was one of the things I noticed most when I left home for the city. Everyone around me smelled like something. Shampoos with names like "argan oil and jasmine" and "sea minerals and sage." Body wash that left a scent trail in the stairwell. Laundry detergent that announced itself from across the room. I'd grown up in a house where clean had no smell at all — just the absence of dirt and the faint residual earthiness of a plant that grew on a tree. For years I thought the absence of scent meant something was missing. Now I think the opposite. My grandmother didn't try to smell like anything but herself, and the soap pods didn't try to change her. They just cleaned, quietly, and stepped out of the way.

Why this habit disappeared

No one banned soap pods. No authority declared them obsolete. They simply faded — crowded out of kitchens by bottles that were easier, foam that was fluffier, scents that were designed in laboratories, and the quiet social pressure of not wanting to be the only household in the apartment building still soaking brown pods in a bowl when everyone else had a plastic bottle that smelled like a tropical vacation.

In the 1980s and 1990s, as Chinese households gained access to affordable shampoos and liquid soaps — first domestic brands, then imported ones — the soap pod bundle disappeared from kitchens. A bottle of shampoo was faster. It didn't need to be broken and steeped and dried and reused. It came in a shape that looked modern on a shelf. The old way didn't die because it was worse. It died because the new way was shinier, and because everyone around you was switching, and because it's genuinely hard to keep doing something the old way when the whole world tells you the new way is progress.

By the time I was a teenager, my grandmother was the only person I knew who still used soap pods. She didn't switch — partly out of habit, partly out of a conviction that the old way worked fine, and partly because she couldn't understand spending money on something a tree produced for free. But she was the last generation. The nail by the sink is still there, thirty years later. Nothing hangs from it now.

I tried it once at my own sink

Years later, living in a city apartment where every cleaning product came in a different plastic bottle with a different colored label, I ordered a bundle of dried soap pods from an online herbal shop. They arrived in a cardboard box — farm-direct, the listing said, from an old honey locust grove in Henan. I untied the twine. The pods were exactly as I remembered them: long, dark, curved, brittle, smelling faintly of hay and nothing else.

I broke off a piece. Steeped it in hot water for ten minutes. Swished. A fine, quiet lather rose to the surface — amber-tinted, dense, exactly as I remembered. I washed my hands. They felt clean. I washed a plate. Same. I stood at the sink for a long moment, holding a wet piece of soap pod in my palm, and felt the past press against the present — my grandmother's kitchen and mine, her hands and my hands, the same gesture separated by thirty years and an entire way of life.

I didn't switch permanently. I still have shampoo in my bathroom and a bottle of dish soap by my sink. But the bundle of zao jiao is still in my cupboard, and every now and then — maybe once a month — I break off a piece, steep it in a bowl of hot water, and wash my hands the old way. Not because it's better. Not because I'm trying to make a point about natural living. Just because it reminds me of someone, and the sensation of lather from a tree pod on a wet palm is worth remembering. Dried mugwort in a foot basin gave me the same feeling when I tried that years later — the past carried in a gesture, not a story.

Materials

  • Dried soap pods (Gleditsia sinensis, a.k.a. zao jiao, 皂角) — long, dark brown, curved or straight pods of the honey locust tree. Available at Chinese herbal shops, traditional medicine halls, and online. Look for pods that are intact, dark brown to nearly black, brittle when dry, and free of mold or insect damage.
  • Hot water — just off the boil works best for releasing saponin quickly. Warm water for gentler use.
  • A bowl or basin — any clean container. My grandmother used an enamel basin with a chip in the rim.
  • Optional: a small dish or saucer — for keeping a used pod section in water at the hand-washing station.

Steps — The basic hot-steep method

  1. Break a section — about two knuckles long — from a dried soap pod. The pod snaps cleanly when dry.
  2. Place the section in a bowl or basin. Pour hot water over it — enough for whatever task you're doing (a small bowl for hands, a large basin for hair or dishes).
  3. Let it steep for 10–15 minutes. The water will turn amber and feel slightly slippery between your fingers.
  4. Swish the water to build a light lather. Rub the pod piece between your palms if you want more foam.
  5. Use the soap pod water to wash your hands, dishes, or hair. For hair, work the water through from scalp to ends; for dishes, scrub as usual.
  6. After use, set the pod section on a saucer or windowsill to dry. It can be reused for several sessions.
  7. When the pod section no longer produces lather — typically after 4–6 uses — it's spent. Compost it.

A few things to keep in mind

Soap pod water will sting your eyes — the saponin is an irritant to mucous membranes, the same way any soap is. Keep your eyes closed when washing your face, and tilt your head back when rinsing your hair.

Some people find the natural scent of soap pods off-putting. It's earthy, woody, faintly herbal — nothing like the fragranced products most of us are used to. If you've spent decades associating "clean" with floral or citrus perfumes, the smell of plain saponin can register as strange or even unpleasant. That's not a flaw in the pod. It's a difference in expectation.

Soap pod lather is fine and low rather than thick and towering. You may feel, at first, that nothing is happening. It is. The cleaning action of saponin doesn't depend on visible bubbles. The foam is a side effect, not the mechanism.
